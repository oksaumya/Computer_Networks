How the Internet Actually Works

Start here. These four lectures give you the map of the whole course: what the Internet is made of, how data moves through it, why it is sometimes slow, and how the job is split into layers.

Mid-semLecture 1

Lecture 1 — The Internet and the Instagram journey

The Internet is not one big network. It is millions of small networks joined together, and your data hops across many of them to reach a server.

What is a computer network?

A network is two or more devices connected so they can send data to each other. Your phone and your WiFi router form a network. Your college's computers form a bigger one. Connect all such networks in the world to each other and you get the Internet, which is why it is called a network of networks.

Networks are named by how much area they cover:

TypeFull nameSizeExample
PANPersonal Area NetworkA few metresPhone ↔ smartwatch or earbuds over Bluetooth
LANLocal Area NetworkOne home, office, or buildingYour home WiFi; a college computer lab
MANMetropolitan Area NetworkA cityA city-wide network run by a municipality or a large organisation
WANWide Area NetworkCountries and continentsA company linking its branch offices; the Internet itself

What is "the cloud", really?

"The cloud" sounds like something floating in the sky. It is the opposite: huge buildings called data centres, each filled with thousands of physical computers (servers). Instagram's photos, Netflix's movies, and your Google Drive files all sit on real disks in those buildings. Companies such as AWS (Amazon), Azure (Microsoft), and GCP (Google) rent those servers out.

The continents are joined by submarine cables: fibre-optic cables lying on the ocean floor. There were about 597 cable systems in 2025. They are physical objects, so they break. The most common cause is ships' fishing anchors dragging across them.

Networks are not new. In 1969, a computer at UCLA tried to send the word "login" to a computer at Stanford Research Institute. Only "lo" got through before the system crashed. That was the first message on the network that became the Internet.

Two ways to describe the Internet

Exams like to ask for both views. They describe the same thing from two angles.

  • Hosts / end systems: phones, laptops, servers. They sit at the edge and run apps.
  • Communication links: fibre, copper, radio. Each has a speed (bits per second).
  • Packet switches: routers and switches that pass data along.
  • ISPs: Internet Service Providers (Jio, Airtel) that connect you to everyone else.
  • Protocols: the agreed rules (HTTP, TCP, IP) for how to talk.
  • The Internet is an infrastructure that provides services to applications: web, video, email, games.
  • An app does not care about cables. It just asks the Internet "deliver this data to that app on that computer".
  • The Internet offers two main delivery services: reliable (TCP) and fast-but-unreliable (UDP). You will meet them in part 3.

A protocol is a set of rules that says what messages look like, what order they are sent in, and what each side does when it gets one. People do this too: you say "Hi", the other person says "Hi", then you ask your question. If someone replies in a language you do not know, the conversation fails. Computers are the same.

Network edge and network core

Split the Internet into three parts:

PartWhat is thereIts job

A server in a Google data centre is at the edge, not the core. "Edge" means "an end point where data starts or finishes", not "small device". The core contains only routers and links that pass data through.

The Instagram journey

The question from class: you tap the Instagram icon and a reel plays in under a second. What did your data touch on the way? Most people say "WiFi → Instagram". The real answer is much longer, and it is the outline of this whole course.

What happens, step by step. Each step is a later topic, so do not worry if a word is new.

  1. Find the address. Your phone only knows the name instagram.com. It asks DNS for the matching IP address (a number such as 157.240.x.x). → DNS
  2. Open a connection. Your phone and the server do a TCP 3-way handshake to agree to talk. → TCP
  3. Lock it. A TLS handshake sets up encryption so nobody in between can read your data. → HTTPS/TLS
  4. Ask for the reel. The app sends an HTTP request: "GET this video". → HTTP
  5. Chop and send. The request is cut into packets. Each packet hops from router to router through the access network, the ISP, and the core. → packet switching
  6. Serve from nearby. The video usually comes from a CDN server in or near your city, not from America. → CDN
  7. Come back reliably. The video returns as thousands of packets. TCP puts them in order and re-sends any that got lost. → reliable delivery

traceroute: see the hops yourself

traceroute is a command that shows every router your data passes through on the way to a destination, and how long each one takes to answer. Each router on the path is called a hop.

# Mac / Linux:  traceroute youtube.com      Windows:  tracert youtube.com
 1  192.168.1.1      2.1 ms    ← your home WiFi router (access network)
 2  10.50.0.1        9.8 ms    ← first router at your ISP
 3  core1.isp.net   14.3 ms    ← ISP core
 4  core2.isp.net   22.9 ms    ← still in the core, farther away
 5  * * *                      ← this router chose not to answer
 6  142.250.x.x     28.4 ms    ← Google's server (destination)

How to read it:

  • The number on the left is the hop number. The time is the round trip from your computer to that hop and back.
  • Latency by hop: the first hop (your own router) is tiny. The time jumps when you leave your home (access network → ISP), then grows slowly through the core. A big jump usually means a long physical distance, such as an undersea cable.
  • * * * means that router did not reply. That is normal; many routers are set to ignore traceroute. It does not mean the path is broken.
  • If the final time is small (say 10–30 ms) for a site hosted abroad, you are being served by a CDN server near you.

How traceroute works inside (the TTL trick) is in Lecture 16.

The course starts with the things you already use (web, HTTP, DNS, streaming) and then goes down: Application → Transport (TCP/UDP) → Network (IP, routing) → Link → Physical. Knowing what apps need first explains why the lower layers are built the way they are.

Check yourself

Why is the Internet called a "network of networks"?

Because it is not a single network owned by one body. It is millions of separate networks (home, college, company, ISP) connected to each other through ISPs so that any host can reach any other.

Is a web server at the network edge or in the core?

At the edge. Every end system (client or server) is at the edge. The core contains only routers and the links between them.

Name three access network technologies.

WiFi, cellular (4G/5G), Ethernet. Home fibre, cable, and DSL also count.

In a traceroute, hop 5 shows * * * but hop 6 answers normally. Is the network broken at hop 5?

No. Hop 5 forwarded your packets (otherwise hop 6 could not reply). It just refused to send a reply to traceroute.

What is the difference between the nuts-and-bolts view and the service view?

Nuts-and-bolts lists the parts: hosts, links, routers/switches, ISPs, protocols. The service view describes the Internet as an infrastructure that delivers data for applications.

Lecture 2 — Packet switching, delay, loss, throughput

Data is cut into small packets that share the links with everyone else's packets. Sharing is efficient, but it means packets can be delayed or dropped.

Circuit switching vs packet switching

Landline call (circuit switching): a line is reserved for the whole call. If grandma pauses for 10 seconds to think, the line sits idle. It is reserved but wasted, and nobody else can use it.

WhatsApp call (packet switching): if you pause for 10 seconds, no packets are sent. That capacity is free for someone else.

Circuit switching is the old telephone model. Before any talking starts, the network reserves a dedicated path from end to end and holds it for the whole call.

Packet switching is how the Internet works. Nothing is reserved. Your message (a photo, a web page, a voice clip) is chopped into small chunks called packets. Each packet has:

  • a header: addressing information (where it is from, where it is going), and
  • a payload: a piece of your actual data.

Each packet is forwarded on its own, hop by hop, router to router. Two packets of the same photo may even take different routes.

Two rules of packet switching you must know

1. Store-and-forward. A router must receive the entire packet before it sends even the first bit onward: receive fully → check → forward. No router needs to know the whole path. It only needs to know the next hop.

2. Statistical multiplexing. "Multiplexing" means many users sharing one link. "Statistical" means the sharing is on demand, with no fixed slots. It works because real traffic is bursty: you send a lot for a moment, then nothing while you read. It is statistically unlikely that everyone bursts at the same instant, so one link can serve many more users than circuit switching could.

This gives packet switching two strengths: efficiency (links are used only when needed) and resilience (nothing is reserved on a path, so traffic can go around a failed link).

Packet switching gives no built-in guarantees. A packet can wait behind others in a queue, or be dropped when a router's memory is full. TCP (Lectures 9–11) exists to hide this: it detects loss, re-sends, and paces the data so that an unreliable core feels reliable to your app.

The four kinds of delay

Every packet, at every router on its path, pays four delays.

A caravan of cars arrives at a toll booth, then drives down a highway to the next toll booth. The cars are the bits of one packet. The booth is the router's outgoing link. The highway is the cable.

Toll booth = transmission. Each car passes the booth one at a time. More cars (a bigger packet) or a slower booth (a slower link) means a longer wait.

Highway = propagation. Once through, cars drive at the speed limit. The drive time depends only on the distance and the speed limit, not on how many cars there are.

Transmission delay = L / R

dtrans = L / RL = packet length in bits · R = link rate in bits per second

A packet of L = 8,000 bits is sent on a link of R = 2 Mbps.

Step 1: put R in bits per second. 2 Mbps = 2,000,000 bps.
Step 2: divide. 8,000 ÷ 2,000,000 = 0.004 s = 4 ms.

Double the link rate and the delay halves: 2 Mbps → 4 ms, 4 Mbps → 2 ms, 8 Mbps → 1 ms.

Propagation delay = d / s

dprop = d / sd = length of the link in metres · s = signal speed, about 2 × 10⁸ m/s in fibre or copper

A fibre link from Delhi to Mumbai is about 1,400 km long.

Step 1: put d in metres. 1,400 km = 1,400,000 m.
Step 2: divide. 1,400,000 ÷ 200,000,000 = 0.007 s = 7 ms.

A faster link (more Mbps) does not change this. Only a shorter distance does.

A geostationary satellite link is sold as 500 Mbps, yet a video call on it has an awkward half-second lag. Why?

The satellite is about 35,786 km above the equator. Your signal goes up and comes back down: roughly 71,500 km. Radio in space travels at the speed of light, 3 × 10⁸ m/s.

71,500,000 m ÷ 300,000,000 m/s ≈ 0.24 s = about 240 ms, from physics alone. The other person's reply needs the same again, which is where the half-second comes from. The 500 Mbps only shrinks transmission delay. It cannot shrink propagation delay.

A 1,500-byte packet crosses one link of 10 Mbps that is 2,000 km long (fibre). Ignore processing and queuing. Find the delay.

Units first: 1,500 bytes × 8 = 12,000 bits. 10 Mbps = 10,000,000 bps. 2,000 km = 2,000,000 m.

Transmission = 12,000 ÷ 10,000,000 = 0.0012 s = 1.2 ms.
Propagation = 2,000,000 ÷ 200,000,000 = 0.01 s = 10 ms.
Total = 1.2 + 10 = 11.2 ms.

  • Bytes vs bits. 1 byte = 8 bits. Packet sizes are often given in bytes; link rates are always in bits per second. Convert before dividing.
  • k, M, G in rates are powers of 10: 1 kbps = 10³, 1 Mbps = 10⁶, 1 Gbps = 10⁹ bps.
  • km → m (× 1,000) and seconds → ms (× 1,000).

Packet loss

A router keeps waiting packets in a buffer (also called a queue). The buffer has a fixed size. If packets arrive faster than the outgoing link can send them, the queue grows. When the buffer is full, the next packet to arrive has nowhere to go and is dropped. That is packet loss.

A lost packet may be re-sent by the sender (TCP does this) or simply stay lost (UDP). You only need the idea for the exam, not any queue maths.

Throughput and the bottleneck link

Throughput is the rate (bits per second) at which data actually arrives at the receiver. It is what you really get, not what is advertised.

A path is a chain of links. Data cannot flow through the chain faster than its slowest link. That slowest link is the bottleneck link.

end-to-end throughput ≈ min(R₁, R₂, …, Rₙ)

You download a 6 MB file over the path above. How long does it take (roughly)?

File size in bits: 6 MB = 6 × 8 = 48 Mb. Throughput = min(10, 2, 5) = 2 Mbps. Time = 48 ÷ 2 = 24 seconds.

Path: Netflix server → a congested link between networks → your ISP → home WiFi → your TV. The 300 Mbps is only your access link to the ISP. It is one stop on a long path. The real bottleneck may be a congested link between networks, an overloaded server, or very often your own WiFi struggling with walls and interference. When a stream buffers, ask: which hop is the slowest?

Bandwidth / link rate is the maximum a link can carry. Throughput is what you actually receive end to end. Delay is how long one packet takes. A path can have high throughput and still high delay (the satellite link).

Check yourself

A 1,000-byte packet is sent on a 4 Mbps link. What is the transmission delay?

L = 1,000 × 8 = 8,000 bits. R = 4,000,000 bps. 8,000 ÷ 4,000,000 = 0.002 s = 2 ms.

A fibre link is 600 km long. What is the propagation delay?

600,000 m ÷ 2 × 10⁸ m/s = 0.003 s = 3 ms.

You upgrade a link from 10 Mbps to 100 Mbps. Which delay changes, and which does not?

Transmission delay becomes 10 times smaller (L/R). Propagation delay does not change, because the distance is the same.

A path has links of 50 Mbps, 8 Mbps, 100 Mbps, and 20 Mbps. What is the end-to-end throughput?

8 Mbps, the minimum. The 8 Mbps link is the bottleneck.

Why is packet switching better than circuit switching for web browsing?

Web traffic is bursty: a short burst when a page loads, then silence while you read. Circuit switching would keep a path reserved and idle during the silence. Packet switching lets other users use that capacity.

What does "store-and-forward" mean?

A router must receive the whole packet before it starts sending it on the next link.

What causes packet loss?

A router's buffer is finite. When packets arrive faster than they can be sent out and the buffer is full, arriving packets are dropped.

Lecture 3 — Protocol layers: OSI vs TCP/IP

Networking is too big to solve as one problem, so it is split into layers. Each layer does one job and talks only to the layer directly above and below it.

Why layering?

You write a letter, put it in an envelope, and give it to the postal service. Does the mail carrier care what the letter says? No. The carrier needs only the address on the outside. You need to know how to write a letter and nothing about sorting offices or flight routes. Neither of you understands the other's job, and the letter still arrives.

If the carrier did read the letter, your privacy would be gone and the carrier would have to understand every kind of message. A layer that looks inside the layer above it stops being a clean layer. This idea comes back with encryption.

Three words from the syllabus:

  • Service: what a layer does for the layer above it. Example: the transport layer offers "reliable delivery".
  • Interface: how the layer above asks for that service. It is the boundary between two layers on the same machine.
  • Protocol: the rules a layer uses to talk to the same layer on the other machine. Example: TCP on your phone talks to TCP on the server.

What layering buys us:

  • Modularity. Each layer solves one problem and can be understood alone.
  • Replaceability. Swap WiFi for Ethernet and nothing above needs rewriting.
  • Interoperability. Different companies can build different layers and still work together, because the boundaries are agreed in advance.
  • Easier debugging. When something breaks you can find which layer has the fault.

The OSI model (7 layers)

OSI = Open Systems Interconnection, developed by the ISO (International Organization for Standardization). It is a conceptual model: the model we use to think, teach, and talk ("that's a Layer 4 issue").

Top-down (7 → 1): All People Seem To Need Data Processing — Application, Presentation, Session, Transport, Network, Data Link, Physical.

Bottom-up (1 → 7): Please Do Not Throw Sausage Pizza Away.

The TCP/IP model

The TCP/IP model is the one the Internet actually runs on. It was developed by DARPA (the US Defense Advanced Research Projects Agency). OSI is the model we think with; TCP/IP is the working system.

Encapsulation and decapsulation

Like a set of nesting dolls: each layer puts the data from the layer above inside its own wrapper. Or like gift wrap: paper, then a box, then ribbon going out, and you unwrap in exactly the reverse order on receipt. You cannot untie the ribbon last.

Encapsulation (sender, going down the layers): each layer adds its own header in front of what it received from above.

Decapsulation (receiver, going up): each layer removes its own header and passes the rest upward.

The important property is symmetry: the network layer on the receiver reads exactly the header that the network layer on the sender wrote, and does not touch anything belonging to another layer. This is also why a packet captured in Wireshark looks like nested headers followed by your data.

Which device works at which layer?

A device "operates at layer N" if N is the highest layer whose header it reads.

Lecture 4 — Network devices, topologies, and AWS VPC

Each networking device is defined by how deep into the packet it looks: a hub looks at nothing, a switch at the MAC address, a router at the IP address.

Why do we need network devices at all?

Suppose every computer must reach every other, and you join each pair with its own cable. For n devices you need:

cables = n (n − 1) / 2
Devices (n)CalculationCables
33 × 2 / 23
1010 × 9 / 245
100100 × 99 / 24,950
1,0001,000 × 999 / 2499,500

Ten times the devices needs about a hundred times the cabling. This cannot work in a real office.

The fix: instead of connecting everyone to everyone, connect everyone to one device in the middle. Now n devices need only n cables. The rest of this lecture is about what that middle device should be.

The devices

Hub — Layer 1 (the 1980s answer)

  • Takes the signal arriving on one port and repeats it out of every other port.
  • Does not understand MAC addresses or anything else.
  • Problem (asked in class): unnecessary traffic. Every machine receives data meant for one machine, and while that traffic fills the shared wire, nobody else can send. More devices make it worse.

Switch — Layer 2 (the 1990s answer)

  • Connects devices inside one LAN and forwards a frame only to the correct port, using MAC addresses.
  • "A hub with a memory": it learns which MAC address is behind which port.
  • Has no understanding of IP, so it cannot reach another network.

Router — Layer 3

  • Routes data between different networks using IP addresses.
  • This is the GPS from Lecture 3: it knows routes to places beyond the local link.

Firewall — Layers 3–4 (and up to 7)

  • A security device that watches traffic entering and leaving a network and lets only authorised traffic through.
  • Basic packet filtering decides using four values: source IP, destination IP (Layer 3), source port, destination port (Layer 4). It does not examine the data. More advanced firewalls look higher, up to Layer 7.

Load balancer — Layer 4 or Layer 7

  • Spreads incoming requests across several servers so no single server is overwhelmed.
  • Link to Lecture 2: it is what you build when the bottleneck is a server instead of a link.
  • Layer 4 version = forwards by IP and port. Layer 7 version = reads HTTP. Details in Lecture 12.

Collision domains and broadcast domains

A light-touch topic, but a common short question.

  • Collision domain: a group of devices where, if two send at the same moment, their signals crash into each other.
  • Broadcast domain: a group of devices that all receive a broadcast message (one addressed to "everyone").
DeviceCollision domainsBroadcast domains
HubAll ports share oneOne
SwitchOne per port (it breaks them up)One (it still forwards broadcasts to all ports)
RouterOne per portOne per port (it does not forward broadcasts)

Short version: switches break collision domains; routers break broadcast domains; hubs break neither.

A router has 2 ports. Each port goes to a switch with 4 PCs. How many collision and broadcast domains?

Broadcast domains = 2 (one per router port). Collision domains = one for every switch port in use: each switch has 4 PC ports + 1 port to the router = 5, so 5 + 5 = 10.

The cloud network: Region, AZ, Edge Location, VPC

In the cloud the logic stays the same (same layers, same devices, same topologies). Only the way you build it changes: it is software-defined.

TraditionalCloud era
Physical boxVirtual instance
Copper cablesVirtualised overlays
Manual wiringAPI calls or console clicks
  • Region: an isolated geographic area (for example Mumbai) with several data-centre clusters. There are 30+ worldwide, joined by AWS's own fast backbone.
  • Availability Zone (AZ): one or more separate data centres with their own power and networking. It is an isolated fault domain. Deploy in two or more AZs and your app survives a fire, flood, or power failure at one site.
  • Edge Location: a CDN endpoint that caches content close to users. It shortens d in d/s, so propagation delay falls (Lecture 2 made real).

VPC (Virtual Private Cloud): your own private, software-defined network inside AWS. It is logically isolated from other customers. You choose the IP address range, the subnets, and the routing.

  • A subnet is a slice of the VPC's address range. Each subnet lives in exactly one AZ.
  • A route table is a list of rules saying where traffic goes.
  • An Internet Gateway (IGW) is the door between the VPC and the Internet.
  • One default VPC per Region, with the address range 172.31.0.0/16.
  • One default subnet in each AZ (each a /20). They are public subnets.
  • A main route table with two routes: the VPC's own range → local, and 0.0.0.0/0 (everything else) → the Internet Gateway.
  • An Internet Gateway already attached.
  • A default security group and a default network ACL (firewalls; covered in part 5).

The /16 and /20 are CIDR notation, explained in Lecture 13. For now: you are given a block of addresses and you decide how to cut it up.

How many cables are needed to connect 20 computers directly to each other (full mesh)?

20 × 19 / 2 = 190.

What is the main problem with a hub?

It broadcasts every incoming signal to all ports, so every device gets traffic not meant for it and the shared medium is busy. Congestion grows as devices are added.

Why can't a switch connect two different networks?

It works at Layer 2 and only understands MAC addresses. Reaching another network needs IP addresses, which is the router's job (Layer 3).

Which device breaks up broadcast domains?

A router. A switch breaks up collision domains only.

Star vs mesh: which is cheaper, and which is more fault tolerant?

Star is cheaper (n links). Mesh is more fault tolerant (many alternative paths) but needs n(n−1)/2 links.

What is the difference between a Region and an Availability Zone?

A Region is a geographic area. An AZ is one or more isolated data centres inside a Region. A Region contains several AZs.

What is a VPC?

A private, logically isolated, software-defined network inside AWS where you control the IP range, subnets, and routing.

Which four values does a basic packet-filtering firewall check?

Source IP, destination IP, source port, destination port.
